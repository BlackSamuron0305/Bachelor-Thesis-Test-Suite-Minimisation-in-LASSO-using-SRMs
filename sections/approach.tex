\chapter{Comparative Evaluation and Selection}
\label{chap:comparison}

This chapter evaluates candidate test-suite minimisation techniques against the criteria defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} and justifies the selection of the Mutation-Aware Enhanced Harrold--Gupta--Soffa (MA-EHGS) algorithm for integration into LASSO. The goal is not to re-explain algorithms (Chapter~\ref{chap:literature-review} provides that) but to determine which techniques offer the best trade-offs for LASSO's Stimulus--Response Matrix (SRM) setting. Numerical scores and tables appear in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-tables}, and here we focus on qualitative conclusions.

The evaluation considers the following families of techniques (as introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature-review}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Greedy heuristics:} classical greedy, HGS, Enhanced HGS (EHGS), Delayed Greedy (DG), GE/GRE, and mutation-based greedy variants.
  \item \textbf{Exact optimisation:} Integer Linear Programming (ILP), Boolean Satisfiability Problem (SAT)/constraint encodings and nonlinear formulations such as NEMO.
  \item \textbf{Search-based approaches:} Genetic Algorithms (GAs) and Many-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs), including mutation-aware variants.
  \item \textbf{Data-driven and similarity-based methods:} algorithms that exploit coverage similarity and overlap, particularly under time constraints.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid structural techniques:} REDUNET, which combines set-cover reasoning with CFG analysis.
\end{itemize}

All methods are evaluated along criteria C1--C6: SRM compatibility, coverage preservation, reduction effectiveness, mutation-score preservation, scalability and implementation feasibility.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C1: SRM Compatibility}

C1 requires algorithms to operate directly on SRMs without relying on program-structure analyses such as CFGs or ASTs. Most examined techniques satisfy this.

Classical greedy heuristics, HGS, EHGS and DG all operate on test--requirement incidence matrices~\cite{harrold1993,chen2005,yoo2012,noemmer2020}. ILP and SAT formulations similarly encode set cover over tests and requirements~\cite{rothermel1998,yoo2012,elbaum2002}. NEMO adds multi-criteria optimisation but retains the same matrix representation~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based techniques represent each candidate solution as a subset of tests defined over coverage and mutation vectors~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017}. Similarity- and overlap-based approaches also rely on coverage vectors~\cite{bach2017,khan2020}.

By contrast, REDUNET depends on control-flow graphs and associated program structures~\cite{mongiovi2020}, which LASSO’s SRM abstraction does not provide. It therefore violates C1.

\textbf{Conclusion (C1).} All algorithms except REDUNET satisfy C1. C1 thus serves mainly as an exclusion filter.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C2: Coverage Preservation}

C2 requires that minimisation preserve structural coverage under adequate settings. Greedy SRM-based methods are straightforward to implement: they operate on coverage matrices, require no external solvers, and can be implemented as deterministic procedures with few parameters~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
EHGS and DG extend this pattern with additional heuristic steps but still follow the same basic matrix-based design~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015}.
Integrating such algorithms into LASSO amounts to operating over the SRM representation already present in the system~\cite{Kessel2022}.

Exact ILP/SAT encodings guarantee coverage preservation whenever a feasible solution is found~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO also enforces coverage constraints while optimising additional criteria~\cite{lin2018}.

Search-based methods may either enforce coverage as a hard constraint or treat it as one objective among several~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017}. Similarly, similarity-based approaches typically optimise coverage under time budgets rather than strict adequacy~\cite{bach2017}. Only hard-constraint configurations satisfy LASSO’s C2.

\textbf{Conclusion (C2).} Greedy heuristics, ILP/SAT and NEMO satisfy C2 under adequate settings. Evolutionary and similarity-based methods can satisfy C2 but only when configured to enforce coverage strictly.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C3: Reduction Effectiveness}

C3 evaluates how strongly an algorithm reduces the test suite while meeting C2.

Exact ILP and SAT encodings compute minimal hitting sets for the modelled constraints~\cite{rothermel1998,elbaum2002}. NEMO extends this with multi-criteria optimisation and reports substantial improvements over linear ILP baselines~\cite{lin2018}.

Among heuristic methods, several studies show strong performance for greedy variants. Enhanced HGS and Non-Redundant HGS outperform HGS on their benchmarks, achieving average suite-size reductions around 72--74\% compared with 69\% for HGS~\cite{gladston2015}. Jehan and Wotawa report around 70\% reduction for multiple greedy variants under adequate mutation requirements~\cite{jehan2023}. Delayed Greedy guarantees reductions no worse than classical greedy and often better in practice~\cite{tallam2005}.

Search-based methods such as mutation-aware MOEAs also achieve substantial reductions~\cite{wei2017}, though cross-study comparisons can vary due to differing subjects and evaluation setups.

\textbf{Conclusion (C3).} ILP/SAT/NEMO provide optimal or near-optimal bounds but at higher cost. Among heuristics, Enhanced HGS, Delayed Greedy and related greedy variants consistently achieve strong reduction. \textbf{Conclusion (C3).}
Exact ILP, SAT and NEMO methods provide the strongest guarantees on minimality but at higher computational and integration cost.
Among polynomial-time heuristics, EHGS, DG and related greedy variants offer strong reduction in the reported experiments~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
MA-EHGS builds on EHGS and therefore inherits its basic reduction behaviour, while adding mutation-awareness (C4) without changing the underlying SRM-based selection mechanism.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C4: Mutation Score Preservation}

C4 assesses how well minimisation preserves mutation score. Earlier studies focused mainly on structural coverage~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020}, but recent work incorporates mutation explicitly.

Noemmer and Haas show that coverage-based minimisation can reduce mutation score when coverage thresholds drop below 100\%~\cite{noemmer2020}. Jehan and Wotawa demonstrate that greedy minimisation preserves mutation score when all mutants are required but may lose mutants when requirements are relaxed~\cite{jehan2023}. Wei et al.\ use mutation score as an optimisation objective in MOEAs and show that mutation-aware objectives preserve fault detection while reducing cost~\cite{wei2017}.

MA-EHGS extends Enhanced HGS by treating mutants as additional requirements and weighting them via parameter $\beta$. This provides explicit mutation-awareness while retaining a deterministic, SRM-based heuristic.

\textbf{Conclusion (C4).} Mutation-aware greedy and evolutionary methods provide the strongest mutation preservation. Purely coverage-based heuristics preserve mutation score under adequate settings but not under relaxed ones. MA-EHGS offers a balanced, SRM-compatible mechanism for incorporating mutation information.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C5: Computational Scalability}

C5 examines algorithmic scalability as test-suite and requirement sizes grow. As test-suite minimisation is NP-complete~\cite{garey1979,rothermel1998}, exact methods face inherent limits, whereas heuristics sacrifice optimality for efficiency.

Greedy heuristics scale well and are widely used in practice~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}. Tallam and Gupta show that Delayed Greedy runs in polynomial time~\cite{tallam2005}. Gladston and Nehemiah report practical performance for Enhanced HGS on medium-sized suites~\cite{gladston2015}. Noemmer and Haas confirm scalability on real projects~\cite{noemmer2020}.

Exact ILP/SAT techniques solve small to medium instances but scale poorly as requirements increase~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO handles multi-criteria problems but remains expensive~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based methods incur further cost due to population-based optimisation, requiring many evaluations per run~\cite{wei2017}. Similarity-based methods scale well computationally but target prioritisation under time constraints~\cite{bach2017}.

MA-EHGS matches the scalability of Enhanced HGS, with mutation requirements simply adding more columns to the SRM.

\textbf{Conclusion (C5).} Greedy SRM-based heuristics (classical greedy, HGS, Enhanced HGS, Delayed Greedy, MA-EHGS) offer the best scalability. Exact and evolutionary methods incur substantially higher computational cost.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C6: Implementation Feasibility}

C6 considers practical integration: dependencies, configuration load, determinism and compatibility with LASSO's SRM architecture.

Greedy algorithms are simple to integrate, deterministic and require no external solvers~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}. Enhanced HGS and Delayed Greedy extend this with modest heuristic additions~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015}.

ILP/SAT methods require solver dependencies~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO requires nonlinear optimisation infrastructure~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based methods require evolutionary operators, objective modelling and parameter tuning~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017}, and introduce non-determinism. Similarity-based approaches rely on execution-time metadata and domain-specific similarity choices~\cite{bach2017}.

MA-EHGS remains deterministic, SRM-based and requires only two weighting parameters ($\alpha$, $\beta$).

\textbf{Conclusion (C6).} SRM-based greedy heuristics require the least engineering effort and fit naturally into LASSO. Solver-based, evolutionary and similarity-based techniques involve significantly higher integration complexity.

\section{Selection Decision}

Across C1--C6, several algorithms perform well individually:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C2 (coverage):} classical greedy, HGS, Enhanced HGS, Delayed Greedy, ILP/SAT and NEMO~\cite{harrold1993,rothermel1998,elbaum2002,gladston2015,tallam2005,yoo2012,noemmer2020}.
  \item \textbf{C3 (reduction):} ILP/SAT/NEMO provide optimal reductions. Enhanced HGS and Delayed Greedy achieve strong heuristic reductions~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
  \item \textbf{C4 (mutation):} mutation-based greedy and mutation-guided MOEAs~\cite{jehan2023,wei2017}.
  \item \textbf{C5--C6 (scalability/feasibility):} greedy matrix-based heuristics~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
\end{itemize}

Combining the criteria, classical greedy, Enhanced HGS, Delayed Greedy, mutation-based greedy variants and MA-EHGS all score highly. The key differentiator for this thesis is C4: explicit mutation-awareness in an SRM-compatible, deterministic algorithm.

MA-EHGS is selected because it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item operates directly on SRMs (C1),
  \item preserves structural coverage when $\alpha>0$ (C2),
  \item inherits strong reduction behaviour from Enhanced HGS (C3),
  \item incorporates mutation awareness via weighted mutant requirements (C4),
  \item retains greedy scalability (C5),
  \item and integrates into LASSO without external dependencies (C6).
\end{itemize}

Other algorithms excel on individual criteria—e.g.\ ILP/SAT/NEMO on optimality or MOEAs on multi-objective flexibility—but at the cost of scalability or complexity. Purely coverage-based greedy heuristics perform well on C2, C3, C5 and C6 but lack mutation-awareness (C4).

\textbf{Overall,} MA-EHGS provides the most favourable balance of SRM compatibility, coverage preservation, reduction effectiveness, mutation-awareness, scalability and implementation feasibility for LASSO. The following chapter presents its implementation and empirical evaluation.

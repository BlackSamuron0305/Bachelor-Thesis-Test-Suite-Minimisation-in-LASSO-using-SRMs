\chapter{Comparative Evaluation and Selection}
\label{chap:comparison}

This chapter evaluates candidate test-suite minimisation techniques against the criteria defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} and justifies the selection of the Mutation-Aware Enhanced Harrold--Gupta--Soffa (MA-EHGS) algorithm for integration into LASSO. The goal is not to re-explain algorithms (Chapter~\ref{chap:literature-review} provides that) but to determine which techniques offer the best trade-offs for LASSO's Stimulus--Response Matrix (SRM) setting. Numerical scores and tables appear in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-tables}, and here we focus on qualitative conclusions.

The evaluation considers the following four families of techniques (as introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature-review}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Greedy heuristics:} Classical Greedy, HGS, Enhanced HGS (EHGS), Delayed Greedy (DG), GE/GRE, and mutation-based greedy variants.
  \item \textbf{Exact and structural optimisation:} Integer Linear Programming (ILP), Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) and nonlinear formulations such as NEMO; this also includes hybrid structural approaches like REDUNET.
  \item \textbf{Search-based and metaheuristic approaches:} Genetic Algorithms (GAs), Many-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs), and mutation-aware variants.
  \item \textbf{Data-driven and similarity-based approaches:} methods exploiting coverage similarity or overlap, typically used under time constraints.
\end{itemize}

All methods are evaluated along criteria C1--C6: SRM compatibility, coverage preservation, reduction effectiveness, mutation-score preservation, scalability and implementation feasibility.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C1: SRM Compatibility}

C1 requires algorithms to operate directly on SRMs without relying on program-structure analyses such as CFGs or ASTs. Most examined techniques satisfy this.

Classical greedy heuristics, HGS, EHGS and DG all operate on test--requirement incidence matrices~\cite{harrold1993,chen2005,yoo2012,noemmer2020}. ILP and SAT formulations encode set cover over tests and requirements~\cite{rothermel1998,yoo2012,elbaum2002}. NEMO retains the same matrix representation~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based techniques represent candidate solutions as subsets of tests defined over coverage and mutation vectors~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017}. Similarity- and overlap-based techniques also rely exclusively on coverage vectors~\cite{bach2017,khan2020}.

REDUNET is the main exception: it depends on control-flow graphs and program-structure analysis~\cite{mongiovi2020}, which LASSO's SRM abstraction does not expose. It therefore violates C1.

\textbf{Conclusion (C1).} All algorithms except REDUNET satisfy C1. C1 thus serves mainly as an exclusion filter.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C2: Coverage Preservation}

C2 requires that minimisation preserve structural coverage under adequate settings. Greedy heuristics guarantee this by construction: they iteratively select tests until all requirements are covered~\cite{harrold1993,chen2005,yoo2012,noemmer2020}. EHGS and DG extend this pattern while maintaining the same guarantee~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015}. 

Exact ILP/SAT encodings ensure coverage preservation whenever a feasible solution is found~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO also enforces coverage constraints~\cite{lin2018}.

Search-based and similarity-based methods may preserve coverage when configured with hard constraints, but many configurations optimise multiple objectives and may trade coverage for other goals~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017,bach2017}.

\textbf{Conclusion (C2).} Greedy heuristics, ILP/SAT and NEMO satisfy C2 under adequate settings. Search-based and similarity-based methods can satisfy C2 but only when configured to enforce it explicitly.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C3: Reduction Effectiveness}

C3 evaluates how strongly an algorithm reduces the suite while meeting C2.

ILP, SAT and NEMO provide optimal or near-optimal reductions~\cite{rothermel1998,elbaum2002,lin2018}. Among polynomial-time heuristics, EHGS, DG and related greedy variants achieve strong reductions: EHGS and GRE often achieve 72--74\% reduction, outperforming HGS at about 69\%~\cite{gladston2015}. Jehan and Wotawa report around 70\% reduction for multiple greedy variants under adequate mutation requirements~\cite{jehan2023}. DG frequently produces the smallest heuristically obtained suites at increased runtime~\cite{tallam2005}.

Mutation-aware MOEAs can also achieve substantial reductions~\cite{wei2017}, though empirical variability is higher due to subject-program and parameter differences.

\textbf{Conclusion (C3).} Exact ILP, SAT and NEMO provide optimal reductions but at much higher cost. Among heuristics, EHGS, DG and similar greedy methods consistently achieve strong reduction. MA-EHGS inherits EHGS's reduction behaviour but adds mutation-awareness (C4).

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C4: Mutation Score Preservation}

C4 measures how well algorithms preserve mutation score. Earlier research focused primarily on structural coverage~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020}, but recent work highlights the importance of mutation-awareness.

Noemmer and Haas show that coverage-only minimisation can lead to mutation-score loss when reduction becomes aggressive~\cite{noemmer2020}. Jehan and Wotawa confirm that under adequate reduction (i.e., 100\% coverage retained), mutation loss is typically small but increases when coverage requirements are relaxed~\cite{jehan2023}. Many-objective evolutionary algorithms explicitly optimising mutation score retain 95--100\% of mutants~\cite{wei2017}.

MA-EHGS incorporates mutation-awareness by treating mutants as explicit requirements weighted by parameter $\beta$, ensuring they influence selection.

\textbf{Conclusion (C4).} Mutation-aware greedy and evolutionary methods provide the strongest mutation preservation. Coverage-only heuristics preserve mutation score under adequate settings but may lose mutants otherwise. MA-EHGS provides an SRM-compatible deterministic method for incorporating mutation information.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C5: Computational Scalability}

Scalability is essential for LASSO, where SRMs may contain hundreds or thousands of tests. Greedy heuristics scale well with $O(nm)$ complexity and are widely used in practice~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}. DG runs in polynomial but higher quadratic cost~\cite{tallam2005}. EHGS performs efficiently on medium-sized suites~\cite{gladston2015}. 

ILP/SAT methods solve only small to medium-sized problem instances~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO is computationally heavier due to nonlinear formulation~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based methods incur significant overhead due to population-based evaluations~\cite{wei2017}. Similarity-based clustering is often $O(n^2m)$ but can perform well in practice for prioritisation tasks~\cite{bach2017}.

MA-EHGS matches EHGS’s $O(nm)$ complexity.

\textbf{Conclusion (C5).} Greedy SRM-based heuristics (classical greedy, HGS, EHGS, DG, MA-EHGS) provide the best scalability. Exact and evolutionary methods incur much higher cost.

%======================================================================
\section{Criterion C6: Implementation Feasibility}

C6 evaluates practical effort, dependencies and integration complexity.

Greedy algorithms are deterministic, simple to implement and require no external solvers~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}. EHGS and DG introduce some heuristic extensions but remain SRM-based and straightforward~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015}. 

ILP/SAT methods require solver dependencies~\cite{elbaum2002,yoo2012}. NEMO requires nonlinear optimisation solvers~\cite{lin2018}. Search-based approaches require evolutionary infrastructure and parameter tuning~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020,wei2017}. Similarity-based methods incorporate clustering or overlap metrics~\cite{bach2017}, adding engineering overhead.

MA-EHGS remains deterministic, SRM-based and requires only the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

\textbf{Conclusion (C6).} SRM-based greedy heuristics require the least implementation effort. Solver-based, evolutionary and similarity-based methods involve substantially higher engineering complexity.

%======================================================================
\section{Selection Decision}

Across C1--C6, several algorithms perform well:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C2 (coverage):} Classical greedy, HGS, EHGS, DG, ILP/SAT and NEMO~\cite{harrold1993,rothermel1998,elbaum2002,gladston2015,tallam2005,yoo2012,noemmer2020}.
  \item \textbf{C3 (reduction):} ILP/SAT/NEMO provide optimal reductions; EHGS and DG provide strong heuristic reductions~\cite{tallam2005,gladston2015,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
  \item \textbf{C4 (mutation):} Mutation-aware greedy and MOEA techniques~\cite{jehan2023,wei2017}.
  \item \textbf{C5--C6 (scalability/feasibility):} Greedy matrix-based heuristics~\cite{yoo2012,noemmer2020,jehan2023}.
\end{itemize}

Combining all criteria, several algorithms are competitive, but MA-EHGS is selected because it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item operates directly on SRMs (C1),
  \item guarantees structural coverage when $\alpha>0$ (C2),
  \item inherits strong reduction behaviour from EHGS (C3),
  \item provides explicit mutation-awareness via weighted mutant requirements (C4),
  \item retains greedy $O(nm)$ scalability (C5),
  \item requires no external solvers or complex infrastructure (C6).
\end{itemize}

Other algorithms excel in individual criteria—e.g.\ ILP/SAT/NEMO on optimality or MOEAs on multi-objective flexibility—but at the cost of scalability or engineering complexity. Coverage-only greedy variants perform well but lack mutation-awareness.

\textbf{Overall,} MA-EHGS provides the most favourable balance of SRM compatibility, coverage preservation, reduction effectiveness, mutation-awareness, scalability and implementation feasibility for LASSO. The following chapter presents its implementation and empirical evaluation.

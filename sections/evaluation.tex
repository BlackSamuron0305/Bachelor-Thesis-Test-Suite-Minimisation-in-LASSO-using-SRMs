\chapter{Evaluation Criteria and Scoring Framework}\label{chap:evaluation}

Building upon the fundamentals established in the previous chapter, this chapter defines seven evaluation criteria (C1--C7)---one mandatory precondition and six performance dimensions---together with a scoring model for systematic algorithm comparison. These criteria operationalise the research questions and provide the foundation for evaluating different minimisation algorithms in the next chapter.

\section{Evaluation Criteria (C1--C7)}

\subsection{Mandatory Precondition}

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{C1: SRM Compatibility (Mandatory)}] 
  The algorithm must operate directly on \emph{Stimulus--Response Matrix} (SRM) representations without requiring control-flow graphs, syntax trees, or language-specific structural analysis~\cite{Kessel2022}. 
  Algorithms failing C1 receive a score of zero and are excluded. 
  This precondition guarantees language-independent execution across heterogeneous software systems.
\end{description}

\subsection{Performance Criteria}

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{C2: Coverage Retention}] 
  Measures how much of the original structural coverage (instruction, line, branch) is retained after minimisation~\cite{zhu1997}. 
  High retention indicates that the reduced suite maintains structural adequacy.

  \item[\textbf{C3: Reduction Effectiveness}] 
  Quantifies the proportion of test cases eliminated while preserving required coverage~\cite{yoo2012}. 
  A high reduction rate reflects strong elimination of redundant tests and tangible computational savings.

  \item[\textbf{C4: Fault Detection Retention}] 
  Evaluates whether the minimised suite preserves the ability to reveal real software defects. 
  Since redundancy with respect to coverage does not imply redundancy with respect to faults~\cite{just2014}, this criterion measures the proportion of defect-revealing tests retained.

  \item[\textbf{C5: Mutation-Coverage Preservation}] 
  Assesses fault-detection capability via mutation testing~\cite{jehan2023,noemmer2020}. 
  The mutation score---ratio of killed mutants to total mutants---acts as a fault-based adequacy metric complementing structural coverage.

  \item[\textbf{C6: Computational Scalability}] 
  Examines algorithm performance on large SRMs as the number of tests and requirements increases. 
  Methods with near-linear complexity and efficient runtime behaviour are favoured~\cite{Kessel2022,bach2017}.

  \item[\textbf{C7: Implementation Feasibility}] 
  Considers practical integration factors such as implementation effort, parameter tuning, dependency overhead, and compatibility with LASSO’s architecture~\cite{yoo2012}. 
  Algorithms imposing heavy maintenance or configuration costs are penalised.
\end{description}

\subsection{Justification of Criteria}
\label{sec:criteria-justification}

The seven criteria align directly with the research questions from Section~\ref{sec:rq}, ensuring coherence between theoretical goals and empirical assessment.

\textbf{C1 (SRM Compatibility)} is mandatory because LASSO operates entirely on SRM abstractions~\cite{Kessel2022}. 
Language independence requires that algorithms process abstract requirement matrices rather than language-specific artefacts.

\textbf{C2--C5 (Quality Preservation)} address adequacy and fault detection. 
\textbf{C2} ensures structural completeness; \textbf{C3} quantifies the degree of reduction; \textbf{C4} safeguards against loss of fault-revealing tests as observed in Defects4J benchmarks~\cite{just2014}; and \textbf{C5} measures mutation-score retention, a strong empirical proxy for test effectiveness~\cite{jehan2023}. 

\textbf{C6 (Computational Scalability)} ensures the algorithm remains feasible for large-scale use cases where SRMs may contain thousands of rows and columns~\cite{Kessel2022,bach2017}. 

\textbf{C7 (Implementation Feasibility)} recognises the cost of practical adoption. 
Even theoretically optimal algorithms may become impractical if they require complex configuration or structural changes to the LASSO pipeline~\cite{yoo2012}.

\section{Scoring Model}

Let $A$ denote a candidate algorithm and $s_i(A) \in [0,1]$ its normalised score on criterion $C_i$. 
Let $w_i \ge 0$ denote the relative weight for criterion $C_i$, with $\sum_{i=2}^{7} w_i = 1$. 
The total score is defined as:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Score}(A) =
  \begin{cases}
    0, & \text{if } s_1(A) = 0 \text{ (fails C1)}, \\[3pt]
    \displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{7} w_i \cdot s_i(A), & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Rank-Based Point Assignment}

Scores $s_i(A)$ for each criterion $C_i$ are derived through rank-based evaluation, following standard regression-testing methodology~\cite{yoo2012}. 
For each performance criterion ($i \in \{2,\ldots,7\}$), algorithms are ranked by performance; the top-ranked algorithm receives the highest score, and tied algorithms share the same rank. 
Subsequent ranks are offset accordingly (e.g., a tie for first shifts the next rank to third). 
This ensures equitable treatment of equivalent performers while maintaining meaningful differentiation across criteria.

\chapter{Evaluation Criteria and Scoring Framework}\label{chap:evaluation}

Building upon the fundamentals established in the previous chapter, this chapter defines six evaluation criteria (C1--C6): one mandatory precondition and five performance dimensions, together with a scoring model for systematic algorithm comparison. These criteria operationalise the research questions and provide the foundation for evaluating different minimisation algorithms in the next chapter.

\section{Evaluation Criteria (C1--C6)}

\subsection{Mandatory Precondition}

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{C1: SRM Compatibility (Mandatory)}] 
  The algorithm must operate directly on \emph{Stimulus--Response Matrix} (SRM) representations without requiring control-flow graphs, syntax trees, or language-specific structural analysis~\cite{Kessel2022}. 
  Algorithms failing C1 receive a score of zero and are excluded. 
  This precondition guarantees language-independent execution across heterogeneous software systems.
\end{description}

\subsection{Performance Criteria}

\begin{description}
  \item[\textbf{C2: Coverage Retention}] 
  Measures how much of the original structural coverage (instruction, line, branch) is retained after minimisation~\cite{zhu1997}. 
  High retention indicates that the reduced suite maintains structural adequacy.

  \item[\textbf{C3: Reduction Effectiveness}] 
  Quantifies the proportion of test cases eliminated while preserving required coverage~\cite{yoo2012}. 
  A high reduction rate reflects strong elimination of redundant tests and tangible computational savings.

  \item[\textbf{C4: Mutation-Coverage Preservation}] 
  Assesses fault-detection capability via mutation testing~\cite{jehan2023,noemmer2020}. 
  The mutation score (ratio of killed mutants to total mutants) acts as a fault-based adequacy metric complementing structural coverage.

  \item[\textbf{C5: Computational Scalability}] 
  Examines algorithm performance on large SRMs as the number of tests and requirements increases. 
  Methods with near-linear complexity and efficient runtime behaviour are favoured~\cite{Kessel2022,bach2017}.

  \item[\textbf{C6: Implementation Feasibility}] 
  Considers practical integration factors such as implementation effort, parameter tuning, dependency overhead, and compatibility with LASSO’s architecture~\cite{yoo2012}. 
  Algorithms imposing heavy maintenance or configuration costs are penalised.
\end{description}

\subsection{Justification of Criteria}
\label{sec:criteria-justification}

The six criteria align directly with the research questions from Section~\ref{sec:rq}, ensuring coherence between theoretical goals and empirical assessment.

\textbf{C1 (SRM Compatibility)} is mandatory because LASSO operates entirely on SRM abstractions~\cite{Kessel2022}. 
Language independence requires that algorithms process abstract requirement matrices rather than language-specific artefacts.

\textbf{C2--C4 (Quality Preservation)} address adequacy and fault detection. 
\textbf{C2} ensures structural completeness (coverage retention). \textbf{C3} quantifies the degree of reduction. \textbf{C4} measures mutation-score retention, a strong empirical proxy for test effectiveness and fault detection capability~\cite{jehan2023}. 
Together, C2 and C4 provide comprehensive assurance that the minimised test suite maintains both structural coverage and fault-revealing capability. 

\textbf{C5 (Computational Scalability)} ensures the algorithm remains feasible for large-scale use cases where SRMs may contain thousands of rows and columns~\cite{Kessel2022,bach2017}. 

\textbf{C6 (Implementation Feasibility)} recognises the cost of practical adoption. 
Even theoretically optimal algorithms may become impractical if they require complex configuration or structural changes to the LASSO pipeline~\cite{yoo2012}.


\section{Ranking and Scoring Model}
To enable systematic comparison, each algorithm is scored on a scale from 0 to 5 for each performance criterion (C2--C6). The scoring rubric is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Score 5:} Excellent performance; meets or exceeds best-known benchmarks in literature.
  \item \textbf{Score 4:} Good performance; achieves strong results with minor limitations.
  \item \textbf{Score 3:} Acceptable performance; meets basic requirements but with notable trade-offs.
  \item \textbf{Score 2:} Poor performance; significant shortcomings limit practical utility.
  \item \textbf{Score 1:} Very poor performance; fails to meet essential criteria.
  \item \textbf{Score 0:} Not applicable; fails mandatory precondition (C1).
\end{itemize}

After scoring, each algorithm's total score is computed as a sum with equal weighting across criteria except C1 (which is mandatory):

\begin{equation}
  \text{Score}(A) =
  \begin{cases}
    0, & \text{if } s_1(A) = 0 \text{ (fails C1)}, \\[3pt]
    \displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{6} w_i \cdot s_i(A), & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

This scoring framework facilitates quantitative comparison across diverse algorithms, enabling informed selection based on empirical performance aligned with LASSO's requirements. 
The next chapter applies this framework to evaluate candidate minimisation techniques from the literature, ultimately selecting the most suitable approach for integration into LASSO.

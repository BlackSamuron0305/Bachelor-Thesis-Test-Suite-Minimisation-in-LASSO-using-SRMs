\chapter{Minimization Algorithms}\label{chap:literature-review}

This chapter surveys test-suite minimisation algorithms from the academic literature. We organise the review by algorithm category, following the taxonomic framework established in Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals}. For each category---greedy heuristics, exact optimisation methods, search-based approaches, and data-driven techniques---we provide a general explanation of the approach and examine selected representative algorithms that illustrate the category's key characteristics and trade-offs.

\section{Greedy Heuristics}

Greedy heuristics iteratively select tests that cover the largest number of currently uncovered requirements until all requirements are satisfied~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020}. These algorithms operate in polynomial time and provide practical solutions with predictable performance characteristics. The fundamental greedy approach can be refined by incorporating additional selection criteria, such as requirement rarity or overlap penalties, to improve coverage retention while maintaining computational efficiency~\cite{harrold1993,bach2017}.

\subsection{Classical Greedy}

The classical greedy algorithm iteratively selects the test case covering the maximum number of currently uncovered requirements, marking all covered requirements as satisfied until complete coverage is achieved~\cite{chvatal1979}. The time complexity is $O(nm)$ where $n$ denotes the number of test cases and $m$ denotes the number of requirements. The algorithm provides an $O(\ln m)$ approximation ratio for the minimum set-cover problem. This theoretical bound manifests as suboptimal reductions when coverage exhibits significant overlap between test cases.

\subsection{Harrold--Gupta--Soffa}

Harrold, Gupta, and Soffa proposed a refined greedy heuristic that prioritises requirements based on their rarity~\cite{harrold1993}. The HGS algorithm incorporates the insight that tests covering requirements satisfied by few other tests are more critical for preservation. Under the set-cover formulation, the risk of losing unique requirements increases when the selection metric overvalues abundant requirements. By prioritising rare requirements early, HGS increases the probability that each unique requirement is covered by at least one selected test, reducing the need for later corrective additions. This strategy preserves the greedy nature and the $O(\ln m)$ approximation ratio inherited from set cover~\cite{chvatal1979} while improving expected retention on practical instances with skewed cardinality distributions.
 
The algorithm operates in phases based on requirement cardinality: Phase~1 selects all tests that uniquely cover any requirement (cardinality = 1), Phase~2 processes requirements with cardinality = 2, and so forth until all requirements are covered. Empirical studies demonstrate that HGS achieves 45--70\,\% reduction while maintaining 95--98\,\% coverage retention~\cite{harrold1993}. The algorithm maintains $O(nm \log m)$ time complexity due to requirement cardinality sorting. Overlap-aware extensions refine HGS by penalising redundant selections and further improve retention without altering the underlying approximation class~\cite{bach2017}.

\subsection{Delayed Greedy}

Tallam and Gupta's delayed greedy algorithm~\cite{tallam2005} uses concept lattices to eliminate implied redundancies before greedy selection. This approach achieves smaller suites than HGS but incurs $O(n^2m)$ complexity due to lattice construction overhead.

\section{Exact Optimisation Approaches}

Exact optimisation methods formulate test-suite minimisation as integer linear programming (ILP) or constraint-satisfaction problems, seeking optimal solutions under specified objective functions~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020}. These approaches guarantee optimality or near-optimality but are NP-hard, often exhibiting exponential runtime growth with problem size. Exact methods may leverage additional structural information, such as control-flow graphs, to improve solution quality, though such inputs are not available in black-box SRM-only settings~\cite{mongiovi2020}.

\subsection{ILP and REDUNET}

Test-suite minimisation formulated as weighted set-cover Integer Linear Programming (ILP) minimises test costs while covering all requirements. Exact formulations produce optimal or near-optimal solutions but are NP-hard, often requiring solver time that grows exponentially with problem size~\cite{yoo2012}. REDUNET~\cite{mongiovi2020} integrates control-flow graph (CFG) analysis into an ILP pipeline, reporting reductions around 90\,\% with near-complete coverage retention. However, REDUNET requires program structure unavailable in SRM-only settings and exhibits exponential worst-case runtime.

\subsection{Constraint and Graph Extensions}

Approaches that enrich optimisation with structural constraints (e.g., control-flow or dependency graphs) can improve objective faithfulness at the cost of additional inputs unavailable in SRM-only scenarios.

\section{Search-Based and Metaheuristic Approaches}

Search-based software engineering treats test-suite minimisation as a multi-objective optimisation problem, balancing competing objectives such as suite size reduction and adequacy preservation~\cite{yoo2012,khan2020}. Metaheuristic algorithms---including genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and particle swarm optimisation---evolve candidate solutions according to fitness functions that encode these trade-offs. These methods provide flexibility and can escape local optima, but require careful parameter tuning and typically exhibit higher computational overhead than greedy heuristics~\cite{hussein2020}.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithms}

Genetic algorithms (GAs) evolve test subsets according to a fitness function that trades coverage retention against suite size~\cite{yoo2012}. GAs provide flexibility but require careful parameter tuning (e.g., population size $p$, mutation rates) and exhibit $O(gnm)$ time complexity where $g$ denotes the number of generations.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired GA}

Hussein et al.\ propose a quantum-inspired genetic algorithm (QIGA) that uses quantum superposition and rotation operators to improve convergence properties relative to classical GAs~\cite{hussein2020}. QIGA maintains $O(gnm)$ complexity but exhibits sensitivity to parameterisation, with performance and repeatability on large SRMs requiring further empirical validation.

\subsection{Simulated Annealing and PSO}

Metaheuristics such as simulated annealing and particle swarm optimisation (PSO) have been explored for test-suite minimisation~\cite{yoo2012}. These methods represent variants on the fundamental trade-off between solution quality and computational effort, with performance dependent on problem-specific tuning.

\section{Data-Driven and Similarity-Based Approaches}

Data-driven methods utilise structural properties of coverage data to identify redundancies and select representative test cases~\cite{bach2017,khan2020}. These approaches analyse similarity or overlap between test coverage vectors, applying clustering, distance metrics, or machine-learning techniques to group related tests and select diverse representatives. Data-driven methods align naturally with SRM-based representations, as they operate directly on coverage information without requiring additional program structure. However, computing pairwise similarities typically incurs $O(n^2 m)$ computational cost, which must be managed in large-scale settings~\cite{bach2017}.

\subsection{Similarity-Based Methods}

Similarity-based methods compute distance metrics (e.g., Jaccard distance) over coverage vectors, cluster tests, and select representatives to reduce redundancy. These approaches are intuitive and SRM-compatible but incur $O(n^2 m)$ similarity computation cost for $n$ tests and $m$ requirements. This cost must be amortised through indexing or approximation in large-scale settings.

\subsection{Overlap-Aware Methods}

Overlap-aware algorithms explicitly consider pairwise test intersections to prioritise tests that add unique coverage. Bach et al.\ report up to 50\,\% higher effectiveness within fixed time budgets relative to classical greedy selection~\cite{bach2017}. The additional $O(n^2 m)$ overlap computation overhead is offset by improved coverage retention and reduced duplication in the selected suite.

\section{Summary and Scope Decision}

Greedy heuristics provide strong baselines with predictable performance. Classical greedy offers $O(nm)$ runtime but over-selects under high overlap~\cite{chvatal1979}. HGS prioritises rare requirements to improve retention at modest additional cost $O(nm\log m)$~\cite{harrold1993}. Delayed greedy further reduces redundancy via lattice-based implication analysis but increases complexity to $O(n^2 m)$~\cite{tallam2005}.

Exact ILP formulations yield optimality guarantees at exponential worst-case cost~\cite{yoo2012}. Hybrid pipelines such as REDUNET leverage CFG information to achieve substantial reductions~\cite{mongiovi2020}, but such structure-dependent inputs do not exist in SRM-only settings and fall outside the feasible design space for LASSO's black-box pipeline. Search-based metaheuristics (GA, QIGA, simulated annealing, PSO) provide flexibility but impose parameter tuning burden and higher computational overhead on large matrices~\cite{yoo2012,hussein2020}. Similarity-based and overlap-aware methods align well with SRM availability and directly target redundancy reduction~\cite{bach2017}.

Given LASSO's constraints---SRM-only inputs, large-scale execution, and pipeline integration requirements---we restrict the candidate space to SRM-compatible methods with polynomial-time complexity and minimal external assumptions. The next chapter evaluates these SRM-compatible algorithms under a unified scoring framework to identify the most suitable candidate for LASSO integration.
